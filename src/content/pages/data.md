## Data Critique

Our dataset contains information about immigration trends across the United States over different years. Each category lays out the total number of people in each state, the rate per million residents, and the state's ranking. Explicitly, the dataset includes information on lawful permanent residents, non-immigrants, refugees, and both affirmative and defensive asylum recipients.

Our dataset illuminates multiple totals based on populations of each state. These categories include the total population, the total number of lawful permanent residents and their relative categories by arrival (new arrivals or adjustments, meaning they were already present in the US and were granted a green card), total number of non-immigrants, the total number of naturalizations, the total number of refugees, and the total number of asylees. Each of these categories is assigned to entries separated by year. Our data set can reveal trends over the years from 2013 to 2022. We can represent each of these categories relative to the total population and/or as a proportion. We can analyze these trends over time to recognize a trend around significant events. These events can include new administrations coming into office, new policies being formed, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Although the State Immigration Data provides us with a lot of useful information, such as statistics, it cannot fully explain immigrants’ personal experiences. Besides that, these data mainly focus on state-level immigration numbers, legal categories, and the changes over time. The dataset also includes permanent residents, naturalizations, refugee arrivals, and asylum grants. To be more specific, I think these statistics are very useful for identifying patterns. In fact, they do not explain the real reasons why people immigrated. One limitation is the lack of individual perspectives, such as immigrants’ family situations and other personal circumstances. To be more specific, immigration patterns in LA may be different from other parts of CA. The dataset’s classification system also raises questions about representation and some facts. For example, the DHS organized immigration information through these official legal documents and administrative categories. These classifications are useful because they do not necessarily reflect how immigrants understand their own identities. They influence the way we interpret the information about immigrants. In conclusion, I think people should recognize these limitations and the missing information, instead of treating government statistics as a complete picture of the problem of immigrants.

The data was generated from administrative records, meaning each entry is produced by a government action rather than a survey. When U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) approves a green card for someone already living in the country, it is recorded as an “adjustment of status.” When someone who received an immigrant visa abroad is admitted at the border by Customs and Border Protection (CBP), it is recorded as a “new arrival.” Together, these make up “Lawful Permanent Residents,” or new green card holders. “Nonimmigrants” are temporary visitors such as tourists, students, and temporary workers, recorded by CBP each time they enter the country, so one person can be counted multiple times. “Naturalizations” are counted when a green card holder takes the oath to become a U.S. citizen. “Refugees” are people screened and approved abroad through the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program and counted when they arrive in the U.S. “Affirmative Asylees” are people granted asylum by USCIS after applying while not in deportation proceedings, and “Defensive Asylees” are people granted asylum by an immigration judge as a defense against deportation. “Combined Asylees” is the sum of the two. The Department of Homeland Security then compiles these records and organizes them by fiscal year and by the state listed on each person’s paperwork.

The original sources of the dataset are the United States Department of Homeland Security’s Office of Homeland Security Statistics (OHSS), United States Customs and Border Protection (CBP), United States Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), and Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE). These are all components used in the department’s immigration data.

The U.S. Congress funded the creation of the dataset by allocating federal discretionary funds as part of the Department of Homeland Security’s budget. There are a few pieces of information that are left out of this spreadsheet. The only missing data from this dataset is some of the rankings in a few columns. The reason for the blank data is that the total amount for these sections is 0, so the ranking is left blank. The columns with this issue, where the total is zero so the ranking is blank, are lawful permanent residents, adjustments, new arrivals, nonimmigrants, naturalizations, and the refugee total. Most of these issues happen in the state of Wyoming, but there are others that are blank and are scattered around the dataset. We will use Breve to clean this issue.

In this dataset, the OHSS uses state and year as the main units of data, with the content being legal events related to immigration. This means that people only appear in the dataset when the government grants them a legal status during that year in a specific state, i.e., a migrant. This also means that people are defined by their legal status, and they don't have a say in how they are categorized. The dataset does not account for people who did not obtain legal status for that year, which puts denials and pending applications out of the picture. It only accounts for where the migrant first got filed. The dataset does not cover movements between states after that. The countries where the migrants are from are also not covered in this dataset. The use of rankings also frames immigration almost as a competition between states and territories. Another significant gap is the exclusion of undocumented immigrants by definition.
