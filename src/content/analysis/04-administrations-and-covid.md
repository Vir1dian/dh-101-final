## Refugees and Asylees by Administration

*By Jai Binning*

[[viz:refugees-asylees-by-administration]]

### Chart type

A line chart works well when we want to see continuous values that change over time. The slope of each line makes trends, peaks, and turning points easy to view and understand. Plotting both asylees and refugees on a shared axis lets us directly compare the two admission pathways (refugees in orange, asylees in blue), and the shaded bands mark the three different administrations (Obama, Trump, and Biden). This allows us to directly check the trends with the specific administration. The horizontal axis shows fiscal year, the vertical axis shows the number of people admitted (0 to 100,000), and a text note marks the 2020 COVID-19 shutdowns. This chart shows us that refugee admissions were fairly high under the Obama administration as refugee admissions peaked at 85,020 in 2016. They then fell down to 22,390 by 2018 in the Trump administration. Asylee numbers, however, were the opposite as admissions in the Obama administration were as low as 20,000, but rose in the Trump administration to as high as 45,580 in 2019. In the year 2020 the COVID-19 pandemic caused travel and processing shutdowns, and both lines kept falling until they reached their lowest points of the 2013-2024 period in 2021. Refugee admissions dropped to 11,880 in 2020 and 11,470 in 2021, and asylum grants fell to 16,360 in 2021. However, both ended up reaching their highest levels in 2024 during the Biden administration as refugee admissions were at 99,990 and asylee admissions were at 68,000.

### Analysis

The diverging lines in the chart show that administration-level immigration is not a single trend. Refugee admissions depend on an annual ceiling that is set by the president, and that ceiling collapsed after 2017 due to decisions by the Trump administration. Asylee grants on the other hand depend on individual cases that move through asylum offices and immigration courts. Executive power reached the two admission pathways in different ways. The rise in asylee grants under Trump may reflect cases filed years earlier finally being decided, rather than a policy choice to admit more people. The 2020 pandemic was a roadblock that made a clean comparison difficult. Travel bans and paused processing pushed both lines down, causing the lowest numbers in this time frame (2013-2024). However, the refugee decline started well before COVID. Refugee admissions had already dropped to 22,390 by 2018, and the 2020 refugee ceiling was set at 18,000 in fall 2019, before the pandemic began. So the 2020 refugee low reflects policy as much as the pandemic, while the asylee drop, which only began after the 2019 peak, lines up more closely with COVID. The numbers after the pandemic shutdown then surged during the Biden administration, although refugee admissions stayed near their low in 2021 and climbed back only gradually, which suggests the resettlement system took years to rebuild after the cuts. These observations just show numbers that changed during the different administrations. The chart doesn’t say why, which is the reason we are required to look at policy documents and secondary sources to better understand who is responsible for these outcomes. This poses a few questions like how much of the asylee rise under Trump came from earlier backlogs and how long does it take to rebuild refugee resettlement capacity once it has been cut?

## Affirmative and Defensive Asylum

*By Melvin Tam*

[[viz:affirmative-vs-defensive-asylum]]

### Chart type

A stacked bar chart worked well for this comparison between affirmative and defensive asylum cases in the United States because it helped show the old administration and new administration response to asylum. This type of chart makes it easier to compare the number of cases in each category (affirmative or defensive) spanning different years, meanwhile showing the combined total of asylum cases. Each bar in the graph represents a year with a separate colored section that shows affirmative and defensive asylum cases. The different colors help differentiate between the two types of asylums. There is also a legend on the right side of the chart to show the asylum color corresponding with the asylum type. In addition to that, I added a feature where the user can drag the years, allowing them to see data based on what year they want to see. Overall, it shows how the balance of the two types of asylum cases changed over time.

### Analysis

The visualization shows that asylum cases increased significantly after 2017 with affirmative and defensive cases climbing and reaching their peak in 2019. Before the new administration in 2017, the total number of asylum cases fluctuated, but was overall stable between 2013 and 2016. After President Trump entered the office, both affirmative and defensive asylum cases jumped to over 45,000 in 2019. Even though affirmative cases were higher than defensive cases, defensive asylum cases were also growing at a faster rate than in past years. This could be that the Trump administration increased immigration enforcement. Furthermore, tough enforcement may have led to the growth of defensive asylum cases, which are filed by a person who is already in deportation proceedings. In Hartsell’s article, “Navigating United States Immigration Courts through Shadowing Asylum Seekers,” she mentioned that the Biden administration and Trump administration handled immigration policy differently. Therefore, there is a big difference in the total number of asylum cases.
